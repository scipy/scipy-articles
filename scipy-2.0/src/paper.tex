\section{When Random is not Good Enough: New Functional for Quasi-Monte Methods}

Both NumPy~\cite{Harris2020} and SciPy provide functionality for generating
(pseudo)random numbers following a multitude of different distributions. These tools
often form the basis of Monte Carlo methods, which are frequently applied to tackle
fundamental tasks such as sampling, estimation, and optimization~\cite{Kroese2014}.
Monte Carlo methods are especially useful when the dimensionality of the problem is
high meaning deterministic methods are infeasible.

A prototypical example is the estimation of the integral
\begin{equation}
    I = \int_{[0,1]^s} f(\mathbf{x})\, \mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},
\end{equation}
which is infeasible to compute via standard quadrature or cubature rules when $s$ is
large. The Monte Carlo method estimates $I$ by
\begin{equation}
    Q = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=0}^{n-1} f(\mathbf{t_i}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{t_i}$ are independent and identically distributed uniform random samples
from $[0,1]^s$. It is well known that, provided $f$ is square integrable, the error of
this estimate is given by
\begin{equation}
    \sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[|I - Q|^2\right]} = \frac{\sigma(f)}{\sqrt{n}},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(f)$ is the square root of the variance of $f$ over $[0,1]^s$. Hence,
we see that the convergence rate is $O(n^{-1/2})$, which, importantly, is independent
of the dimensionality $s$.
