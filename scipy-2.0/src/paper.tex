\section{When Random is not Good Enough: New Functional for Quasi-Monte Methods}

Both NumPy~\cite{Harris2020} and SciPy provide functionality for generating
(pseudo)random numbers following a multitude of different distributions. These tools
often form the basis of Monte Carlo methods, which are frequently applied to tackle
fundamental tasks such as sampling, estimation, and optimization~\cite{Kroese2014}.
Monte Carlo methods are especially useful when the dimensionality of the problem is
high meaning deterministic methods are infeasible.

A prototypical example of this is the approximation of multidimensional integrals,
which is infeasible when computed via standard quadrature or cubature rules if the
dimensionality is large. The Monte Carlo method estimates the integral by evaluating the
integrand at points that are independent and identically distributed uniform random
samples. It is well known that, provided the intergrand is square integrable,
then this approach has a probabilistic error bound of the form $O(n^{-1/2})$, where $n$
is the number of points the intergrand is sampled at. While this
convergence rate has the important property of being independent of the dimension $s$,
it is still rather slow. This led to the development of Quasi-Monte Carlo (QMC) methods,
for which the sample points are chosen deterministically from a
\emph{low-discrepancy sequence} to achieve a faster convergence rate.
This is achieved by choosing points that are more evenly distributed than random points,
avoiding clustering and large gaps that are typical of random points.
For a more detailed discussion and introduction of QMC methods, we refer the reader
to~\cite{Niederreiter1992,Dick2010,Dick2013,Giles2008}.

SciPy 2.0 contains a new submodule, \texttt{scipy.stats.qmc}, which provides
convenient building blocks for implementing QMC methods. It implements
Sobol'~\cite{Sobol1967,Joe2008} and
Halton~\cite{Halton1960,Halton1964} low-discrepancy sequences
